Pairings

vs

Pairing rules applied, in order
  1. Within 200 MMR. Every table is kept inside a 200-point rating window, and the lowest-rated players get the closest matches. If anyone has nobody left inside it, the window widens 50 points at a time for the whole floor until everyone fits; the round says when that happened and marks any table past 200.
  2. Swiss on effective strength, which is the skill level from the brackets sheet adjusted by today's record. One net win is worth half a point, so a 1–0 level 0 is even with a 0–1 level 1 and the two get paired.
  3. Up to one point apart is normal once records diverge. Two or more apart is allowed only when the records cancel the gap, like a 1–0 level 0 against a 0–1 level 2. Three apart is never allowed, so level 0 never sees level 3.
  4. Fewest mirror matchups. Two players on the same expected deck are only paired when nothing else works.
  5. No rematches while a legal fresh opponent exists.
  6. Odd attendance gives one bye, and it goes to an amateur with the lowest record, never twice to the same person. Never-played and noob players are not sat out while any amateur is available. A bye scores nothing, so the sit-out neither helps nor hurts. Where a category has to be crossed to balance the field, the volunteers listed on the Roster tab go first.
  7. Check in “Judge” at level 4 instead of handing out a bye, and the judge gets sent at a level 3 first. Whoever is judging plays under that one placeholder name. Judges never take the bye themselves and stay out of the prizing standings.
  8. Any of this can be overridden. Force a table with the two pickers above the board and it is locked, so a re-roll leaves it alone. Re-roll only reshuffles tables that are neither locked nor already reported, and settled tables keep their numbers.
  9. Someone arriving late is checked in as normal and shows up as waiting for a table. Pairing them in adds tables without touching anything already on the floor; an odd one out is paired against whoever had the bye.
  10. Matches are best of three. Record G1, G2 and G3 and the match result follows the games; pick a side directly to set it by hand. A 2–0 sweep moves MMR 1.25×, a 2–1 only 0.75×, so winning a game off the player who beats you softens the loss.

MMR ladder

Spreadsheet sync

The board is the source of truth during the event; the Triton MMR sheet is the record between events. Copy the ratings out at the end of the night, paste them over A1 in the sheet, and paste the sheet's two columns back in here before the first round.

How the rating moves
  1. Ratings are seeded by skill: 900 + 50 × skill level. Never played opens at 900, noob at 1000, amateur at 1050, and the half steps land in between. The ladder starts out reflecting what the room already knows, so a never-played player beating an amateur reads as the upset it is.
  2. Each match, your expected score comes from the rating gap: E = 1 / (1 + 10^((opponent − you) / 400)). A 100-point underdog is expected to win about 36% of the time, a 200-point underdog about 24%.
  3. The change is K × margin × (result − E). Beating someone rated above you pays more than beating someone below, and the loser pays exactly what the winner earns, so the pool total stays flat.
  4. K is deliberately conservative: 28 for a player's first three matches, then 20. An even matchup moves 12 to 18 points, while a 200-point upset win on a sweep pays about +27. Nobody's rating lurches on one result.
  5. Margin reads the best of three and cuts both ways. A 2–0 sweep was decisive, so it pays 1.25×. A 2–1 could have gone either way, so it counts 0.75×: the winner gains less and the loser drops less. Taking a game off the person who beats you genuinely softens the loss. Both players always use the same multiplier, so the pool stays flat.
  6. A wide gap leans against the higher-rated player. Up to 200 apart a match moves ratings normally. Past that, the favorite wins less and loses more: a win is scaled down to half at 400 apart, and an upset loss is scaled up to 1.5×. The underdog mirrors it, losing less and gaining more, so what one side gains the other gives up. A draw counts as the favorite falling short.
  7. Everyone in a round moves off the ratings they started that round with, so results inside one round never cascade into each other.
  8. A carry-in pasted from the spreadsheet replaces the skill seed for that player, so week two starts where week one finished. Anyone without a carry-in still opens on their skill seed, which is what a first-timer should get.
  9. Seed is where that player started, so MMR minus Seed is what they have earned tonight. The green and red figures beside MMR show exactly that.
  10. Perf is the standard chess performance rating, average opponent + 400 × (W − L) / N: how strong you played tonight judged only by who you actually beat. It ignores your own rating, so it is the fairer read on a short event.
  11. Conf is the uncertainty band, in Glicko's spirit: wide on the first night, narrowing as matches accumulate. A rating marked prov has fewer than three matches behind it and should not be trusted yet.
